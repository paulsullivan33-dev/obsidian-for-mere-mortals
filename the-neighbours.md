# The Neighbours

Outcome: you can say what the two
famous alternatives and one unfamous
one do with your notes that Obsidian
doesn't — and the reverse — well
enough to choose on facts rather
than fashion. These are
characterisations from the tools'
documentation and the shape of their
files, not benchmarks; neither
neighbour was installed in the lab.

## Logseq: the outliner cousin

**Logseq** is the nearest relative:
free and open source, local-first,
and your notes are again plain files
on your own disk. The difference is
the grain. Where Obsidian thinks in
*pages*, Logseq thinks in **blocks** —
every bullet a addressable lump, with
the day's journal page as the front
door and outlines as the native way
of writing. Its links, backlinks and
graph will feel like family; its
query system is built in rather than
plugged in.

Choose it if your thinking arrives
as lists and days, and if *open
source, all the way down* settles
the chapter 1 question more
completely than Obsidian's open-files
answer. Its costs, plainly: your
files carry its outlining habits in
their text, so the notes are plain
but not neutral; and a project whose
centre of gravity has lately been
moving toward a database edition
asks a long-term question Obsidian's
dull folder does not. The two tools
can even read folders in similar
shapes — which tempts experiments
chapter 17 would warn are really
about avoiding a decision.

## Notion: the hosted opposite

**Notion** is the polished opposite
pole: your notes live in the
company's cloud, in pages built from
**blocks** in a database, edited in
a browser or its apps, shared with a
team as naturally as breathing. As a
*collaborative* instrument it is
everything chapter 17 admits
Obsidian is not: permissions,
comments, simultaneous editors, a
manager's idea of a wiki.

Choose it if the notes are a team's
working surface and openness of
format is not the point. Know the
price in this guide's terms: there
is no vault. Export exists, and what
arrives is a one-way snapshot in
files whose structure does not
survive as a living notebook — the
cupboard of chapter 1, with good
lighting and excellent company.
Your words are a service's content,
governed by its terms and its
continuance. For a team's project
hub that can be the right trade. For
a diary it is the trade this whole
guide exists to make visible.

## A plain folder of text files: the control group

The third neighbour has no name to
sell you: the vault *without*
Obsidian — the same Markdown folder,
a text editor you already own, and
search by the tools that have
searched text for fifty years. The
lab ran this neighbour, in effect,
for its first six transcripts: links
audited, tags found, properties
parsed, the whole bookkeeping of the
notebook reproduced with a checker
script and `grep`.

Choose it if your needs are a few
dozen notes, your habits already
live in an editor you love, or your
trust budget for new programs is
spent. Know what you give up, from
the chapters behind you: following
links becomes searching for names;
backlinks become a command you must
remember to run; the properties
panel, the switcher, the graph
become your own scripts. People kept
superb notebooks this way for
decades. The honest summary of this
whole book might be that Obsidian is
what that folder grows when someone
automates its chores — and the
folder, unchanged, is what remains
if the automation ever leaves.

Check: for each neighbour, one
sentence that starts *your notes
would live…* — in files shaped by an
outliner; in a company's cloud, as a
team's surface; in the same folder,
tended by hand. If one of those
sentences describes your life better
than chapter 1's, this guide has
done its work anyway.
