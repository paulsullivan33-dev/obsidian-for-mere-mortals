# Backlinks and the Graph

Outcome: given any note, you can see
what points at it — the reverse of
chapter 5 — and you can open the
graph, read it for the two things it
is genuinely good for, and resist the
third thing it merely looks good for.

## Backlinks: the other direction

You write links forward: this note
mentions that one. The question that
turns a notebook into an instrument
is the reverse: **what mentions this
note?** Those are its **backlinks**,
and Obsidian keeps them without being
asked. Open a note and the backlinks
panel (a core plugin, chapter 12)
lists every note that links here,
with the linking sentence shown, so
you read the reference in context.
The status bar keeps a running count
— the lab's app showed *2 backlinks*
under `Home` and *6 backlinks* under
`Tomatoes`, and the 6 is auditable:
it is the checker's count from
chapter 5, and the rename experiment
in transcript t03 breaks exactly
those six.

Backlinks change how you write. A
note about a thing stops being a
dead end and becomes a junction:
every mention of the tomatoes,
wherever it was written — a daily
note in April, the seed inventory, a
daily note yet to come — collects
under the tomato note when you open
it. You do not file mentions; the
links file them. Two habits make
backlinks earn their keep:

- **Link mentions, not just
  references.** When a daily note
  says the tomatoes went out, write
  `[[Tomatoes]]`, not "the tomatoes".
  The daily note loses nothing and
  the tomato note gains a diary.
- **Open the backlinks before you
  rewrite.** The note you are about
  to "fix" may be load-bearing in
  five arguments you forgot you made.

The same panel offers **outgoing
links** — the forward list, gathered
in one place — and **unlinked
mentions**: places the note's *name*
appears as plain text without a
link. That last list is the app
politely pointing at chapter 5
homework: mentions you could promote
to links with a click, kept honest
by showing you the sentence first.

## The graph: the vault as a map

The **graph view** draws the whole
bookkeeping at once: a dot per note,
a line per link. There is a **global
graph** for the vault and a **local
graph** that shows one note and its
neighbours to a depth you choose. The
lab's global graph is in transcript
t07: nine dots, and the lines are the
26 links of chapter 5 with the
attachments and daily notes standing
exactly where their links put them.

An honest appraisal, because the
graph is the feature in every
screenshot of this app and the one
most likely to disappoint on
schedule:

- **It is genuinely good as an
  x-ray.** Notes with no lines are
  **orphans** — written and never
  woven in. Sometimes an orphan is a
  fine standalone page; often it is a
  note you meant to connect and
  didn't. Clusters show you where
  your thinking actually lives, which
  is not always where you thought.
- **It is genuinely good locally.**
  The local graph around a project
  note is a map of that project you
  did not have to draw.
- **It is not insight.** The lines
  are your links, no more. The graph
  cannot tell a profound connection
  from a passing mention, it grows
  unreadable as vaults grow past a
  few hundred notes, and time spent
  arranging its dots into beauty is
  time spent on a picture of the
  notebook instead of the notebook.
  Chapter 17 returns to this
  temptation; it is the app's most
  charming limit.

Check: open the local graph on a
note you know well. Every line
touching it should be a sentence you
can find with chapter 5's checker or
a text search. The day a line
surprises you, one of the two is
wrong — and it will be the graph,
because the graph is drawn from the
files, not the other way round.
