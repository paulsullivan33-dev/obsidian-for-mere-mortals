# Your First Notes

Outcome: you can write a note, read
it back as a finished page, and find
the note again tomorrow — and you
know which of those acts touched a
file, because all of them did.

## A note is a Markdown file

Click the new-note button (or press
the shortcut your system shows next
to it) and type. What you are writing
is **Markdown**: plain text with a
light dusting of marks — `#` for a
heading, `**two asterisks**` for bold,
`-` for a list — that a reader, human
or program, turns into a formatted
page. If Markdown itself is new to
you, *Markdown for Mere Mortals* in
this series teaches it whole; this
chapter needs only the dusting.

The file is being written as you
type, into the vault, under the name
at the top. There is no save button
because there is nothing to save
*to* — no database behind the
editor. Rename the note in the
explorer and the file renames on
disk. That is not a metaphor, and
chapter 5's experiments lean on it
hard.

## Two ways of looking

The editor shows a note in one of
two tempers, switchable in the
note's top corner and in settings:

- **Editing view** (in its usual
  *live preview* flavour): you write
  in something close to the finished
  page — headings look like headings,
  links look like links — while the
  line you are actually editing shows
  its marks, so the text is never
  pretending to be other than text.
- **Reading view:** the note as a
  finished page, marks hidden, links
  followed by clicking. This is the
  view for *reading* the notebook —
  and the view that cannot lie about
  what a stranger, or a future you
  with a plain editor, would see.

The lab's app opened its notes in the
editing view, and the properties
panel of chapter 7 sat at the top of
`Tomatoes.md` like a title card. The
words underneath were the file's
words, unchanged — the closing diff
in `obsidian-lab/transcripts/t07-appimage-run.txt`
proves the app edited none of them
while looking.

## Habits that pay from the first day

Notes are cheap in Obsidian, and
three small habits compound:

- **One note per subject, named for
  the subject.** `Tomatoes`, not
  `Notes about the garden (final)`.
  Links (next chapter) point at
  names; a stable, obvious name is a
  link that keeps working.
- **Write the link you wish existed.**
  Typing `[[Courgettes]]` in a note
  before the Courgettes note exists
  is not an error — it is a promise
  the app keeps visible, and clicking
  it creates the note. Half the
  structure of a good vault is built
  this way, from inside the writing.
- **Date nothing you can file.**
  Notes about *days* belong to daily
  notes (chapter 9). Notes about
  *things* belong under the thing's
  name, where the links can find
  them.

## The front door

Make one note the vault's **front
door** — the lab's is called `Home` —
and keep in it the short list of
doors into everything else: the
current projects, the standing
references, the note you always lose.
It is an ordinary note; its power is
only that you maintain it. When a
vault grows past the point where the
explorer's list is a map, the front
door is the map you drew on purpose.

Check: pick the note you wrote,
close the app, and open the file —
it is in the vault folder, under its
name with `.md` after it — in any
text editor you already own. Read it.
Everything you wrote is there,
marks and all, and nothing else is.
If that check ever fails, chapter 18
starts there.
