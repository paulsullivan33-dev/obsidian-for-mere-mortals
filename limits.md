# Limits

Outcome: you can recite what Obsidian
cannot do before it has a chance to
teach you by disappointment — and
which of those limits are the price
of the design you chose, rather than
defects awaiting a fix.

## A closed app over open files

The files are open; the program is
not. Obsidian is **proprietary**
software, and this guide's defence of
it has always been the folder, never
the company's permanence. If the
maker vanishes, the app on your disk
keeps working, the vault keeps every
word, and the conveniences decay at
the speed of your operating system's
changes — years, not days. But the
reverse deserves its sentence too:
no community edition can take over,
no one can audit the program that
reads your diary, and features the
maker has not written are features
that do not exist, however many
votes they gather. You are trusting
a small company with the *tool*, and
keeping the *notes* out of the trust
entirely. Hold both halves of that
sentence; dropping either is how
people get hurt by tools they loved.

## The graph knows links, not meaning

Chapter 6's charm is chapter 17's
warning. The graph is drawn from the
links you typed. It cannot weigh
them, date them, or tell a thesis
from a shopping list that mentions
one. In a large vault it becomes
weather — beautiful, patterned, and
about nothing in particular. The
tools that *do* read for meaning
exist, and they read by sending your
words somewhere or by indexing them
in ways this app does not. Obsidian
search is likewise literal: it finds
the word you typed, spelled as you
typed it, and the day you need *the
note about the heap, whatever I
called it*, the answer is the
discipline of chapters 4 to 8 — one
name per thing, kept — not a smarter
search coming in an update.

## One writer at a time

A vault is files, and files have one
editor at a time in their bones.
Sync (chapter 11) moves copies
faithfully and merges nothing: two
people, or one person on two
machines, writing the same note at
the same hour produce a conflict,
not a collaboration. There is no
presence, no comments thread, no
permissions ladder inside the app —
sharing a vault means sharing the
folder, entire, diary and all. Teams
that need a shared writing surface
should hear chapter 16's Notion
paragraph again without flinching:
it is a real need, and this is
really not the tool for it.

## The rest of the honest list

- **Mobile is a smaller room.** The
  phone apps read the same vault,
  but screens shrink, some community
  plugins are desktop-only, and the
  vault's arrival on the phone is
  exactly as good as the sync you
  chose in chapter 11.
- **Attachments are ballast.** The
  app neither shrinks nor transcribes
  them; a vault of recordings is a
  heavy folder wearing a notebook's
  clothes, and every sync pays its
  weight.
- **Properties are a promise you
  keep, not a schema enforced.**
  Nothing stops next month's notes
  quietly dropping a property this
  month's queries depend on.
  Templates are the only police.
- **File recovery is not backup.**
  Snapshots (chapter 12) are recent,
  partial, and stored with the user
  profile — a disk's death takes
  them and the vault together. The
  only backup is a copy somewhere
  else, which is chapter 11 again,
  wearing its other hat.
- **The settings are per vault and
  per machine in awkward halves.**
  `workspace.json` records one
  machine's windows in the folder
  both machines share; chapter 11
  showed the small war that starts.
  It is survivable, and it is a
  limit, and it is yours to know
  about before the war, not after.

And the standing limit beneath the
list, from chapter 1: a vault is as
private as its least private copy.
No feature in this book can outrun
that sentence, and none should be
trusted that claims to.
